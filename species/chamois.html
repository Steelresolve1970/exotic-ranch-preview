Chamois

Rupicapra rupicapra

Overview

Chamois appears on the Exotic Wildlife Association species list as Chamois, Goat (Chamois) — ESA: Not Listed; CITES: Appendix II. [1]

Native range

The range of Rupricapra rupicapra, or chamois, includes the Pyrenees, the mountains of south and central Europe, Turkey, and the Caucasus in Asia. [2]

Size

30–50 kg (ADW range). [2]

Lifespan

14 to 22 years (wild, range); 20 years (wild, average). [2]

Breeding & gestation

Gestation 5.33–6.17 months; offspring per birth: 1–2; females mature at 2.5 to 4 years. [2]

Habitat needs

R. rupricapra lives in alpine and sub alpine meadows above the timberline. It winters in forested areas and steep slopes where snow does not accumulate. ADW biomes: mountains, tundra. [2]

Fencing

Texas Parks & Wildlife states game fences at least 8 ft high (woven-wire or high-tensile) are the only fully reliable deer-exclusion fence. That is the common high-fence standard for deer and antelope; a species-specific minimum for this animal was not found in a primary source. [3]

Natural diet

During the summer months the diet consists chiefly of herbs and flowers, but in winter the chamois eats lichens, mosses, and young pine shoots. ADW diet type: herbivore, folivore. [2]

Feed & supplementation

No verified species-specific protein % or mineral formula was found. Texas deer guidance (16–20% CP pellet; 13–16% CP for growth/reproduction) is sometimes used as a reference point but is not antelope-specific—work with a ruminant nutritionist/exotic vet. [4][5]

Common health issues & parasites

Haemonchus contortus and Longistrongylus dominate abomasal worm burdens in free-ranging exotic antelope in Texas (sable, addax, blesbok studied); captive wild ruminants show anthelmintic resistance, so use fecal egg counts and fecal egg-count reduction tests instead of routine blanket deworming. [6][7]

Vet care & vaccination

No vaccines are labeled for this species in the US; any vaccination protocol is extra-label and should be set by a veterinarian experienced with exotic hoofstock. (No specific protocol was verified for this page.)

Handling & capture

Capture myopathy—muscle damage from pursuit, restraint and transport—is often fatal in wild ungulates. Minimize chase time and stress, avoid hot conditions, use appropriate chemical immobilization by a vet, and release/unload quickly. [8]

Conservation status

IUCN Red List: Least Concern. [9]

Legal notes (general info, not legal advice)

CITES Appendix II (per the EWA list); international trade needs CITES permits. General information, not legal advice. USDA APHIS licensing under the Animal Welfare Act applies to dealers and exhibitors; check USDA and your state wildlife and animal-health agencies before buying, moving or selling. [1][10][11]
