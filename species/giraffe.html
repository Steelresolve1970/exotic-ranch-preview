Giraffe

Giraffa camelopardalis

Overview

Tallest land animal; needs specialist husbandry, especially for cold weather. [1]

Native range

Sub-Saharan Africa. [1]

Size

Bulls up to 1,930 kg, cows up to 1,180 kg; 4.7–5.7 m tall; calves 50–55 kg. [1]

Lifespan

10–15 years wild; 20–25 years captive (record 27). [1]

Breeding & gestation

~457 days (range 400–468); females breed every 20–30 months; weaning 12–16 months. [1]

Temperament & social structure

Loose herds; females mature at 3–4 years, males breed from ~7 years. [1]

Habitat needs

Open savanna/woodland with browse. [1]

Where they thrive (Texas / US)

Suitable anywhere in Texas only with a heated barn: AZA/USDA call for heated housing when temperatures average below 50 °F (inference: this occurs statewide in winter). See the interactive Texas species map (../site/map.html). [2][3]

Space per animal

No verified species-specific stocking rate was found. Carrying capacity depends on rainfall, range condition and competing grazers—get a range/stocking assessment (NRCS or Texas A&M AgriLife).

Housing & shelter

AZA: when average daily temperatures fall below 10 °C (50 °F), provide heated housing that holds at least 18 °C (65 °F), with areas near 24 °C (75 °F); measure at chest height of the smallest giraffe. USDA recommends keeping giraffes indoors in cold, inclement weather; a giraffe died in a drafty barn that was ~70°F up high but 45°F lower down. Acclimatized giraffes tolerate heat up to 49 °C (120 °F) with shade. [2][3]

Fencing

Follow the AZA Giraffe Care Manual for barrier and barn design; no ranch fence-height figure was verified for this page. [2]

Natural diet

Browser; an adult male can eat up to 66 kg of food per day, surviving on ~7 kg in poor areas. [1]

Feed & supplementation

Follow the AZA Giraffe Care Manual nutrition chapter; no protein % was verified for this page. [2]

Common health issues & parasites

Cold stress/hypothermia is a major documented killer; once chilled, a giraffe struggles to regain body temperature without an outside heat source, and its immune system is compromised. Calves and juveniles are at greatest risk. [3]

Vet care & vaccination

No vaccines are labeled for this species in the US; any vaccination protocol is extra-label and should be set by a veterinarian experienced with exotic hoofstock. (No specific protocol was verified for this page.)

Handling & capture

Capture myopathy—muscle damage from pursuit, restraint and transport—is often fatal in wild ungulates. Minimize chase time and stress, avoid hot conditions, use appropriate chemical immobilization by a vet, and release/unload quickly. [4]

Conservation status

IUCN Red List: Vulnerable. [5]

Legal notes (general info, not legal advice)

A USDA AWA exhibitor license is required if exhibited to the public. General information, not legal advice. USDA APHIS licensing under the Animal Welfare Act applies to dealers and exhibitors; check USDA and your state wildlife and animal-health agencies before buying, moving or selling. [6]
